Fragmented Governance and School Funding Inequality in St. Louis

Oleks Zolochevskyi and Nikki Zheng

Washington University in St. Louis.

May 17, 2026

Abstract

The combined St. Louis city-county region contains 115 local governments, 91 municipalities, 81 municipal courts, 60 police departments, 43 fire protection districts, and 65 school districts. This fragmented governance has contributed to a fragmented educational landscape. School inequality in St. Louis is not only a product of governmental fragmentation, but also the result of racial segregation and poverty isolation, both of which are regional problems.

1. Unequal Funding

Educational funding is also distributed unequally. From 2018 to 2019, majority-White districts on average received $1,698 more and spent $2,076 more per pupil than majority-Black districts. The highest-spending majority-White districts, including Clayton and Ladue, spent $8,412 more per student than the highest-spending majority-Black districts, such as St. Louis Public Schools and Ferguson-Florissant, and about $18,000 more per student than the lowest-spending districts, such as Riverview Gardens. These figures reveal substantial disparities in educational resources between White and Black districts, arising from differences in local, state, and federal funding.

2. Local Funding

More specifically, local funding, which makes up the largest share of education funding in Missouri, comes primarily from taxes on residences, farms, and businesses. Another source is sales tax, including state-assessed railroad and utility taxes. However, these funding sources are not distributed equitably across districts because they depend heavily on differences in property wealth. In practice, districts with lower property wealth often have greater educational needs but receive less local funding, making the system regressive. For example, majority-White districts received about 82% of their funding from local sources, compared with 58% for majority-Black districts.

3. State Funding and the Foundation Formula

State funding, which accounts for about 30% of education funding, is also limited in its equalizing effect and can be unstable. Under the Missouri Foundation Formula, the amount a district receives from the state is determined by factors such as weighted average daily attendance, the cost of educating one student, cost-of-living adjustments, and local funding capacity, defined as "local effort." Because local resources are included as one of the components in calculating state aid, state funding can become unstable and insufficient, which makes long-term planning more difficult, especially for higher-need districts that depend more heavily on state support. In terms of the merger proposal, reunification could improve regional coordination, but unless education funding rules are also changed to address the gap between local and state funding across districts, unequal educational opportunity would likely remain.

4. Federal Funding

Finally, federal funds serve mainly as supplements to state and local funding and are intended to support specific groups of students, such as low-income students, students with disabilities, and English language learners. As a result, federal funding alone cannot correct the deeper inequities created by unequal local funding.

5. Conclusion

In summary, historical records show that school inequality in St. Louis has deep roots in racial segregation and unequal access to resources. Therefore, merger alone would not eliminate funding inequality unless it is accompanied by school-finance reform aimed at reducing the major disparities in resources across districts.
